Motivation

What we set out to understand

India's merchandise trade has run a persistent deficit since liberalisation in 1991. Headlines trumpet a growing gap in Rupee terms — but do those numbers reflect physical economic reality, or are they amplified by currency effects and inflation?

We wanted to move beyond surface-level deficit figures to uncover the underlying mechanics: Does currency depreciation help? Is the deficit really an energy problem? Did "Make in India" change anything?

Central Question

What are the true structural drivers of India's persistent merchandise trade deficit, stripped of currency illusion and price distortions?

Our Goals
  • Test classical trade theories in the Indian context
  • Isolate real (inflation-adjusted) trends from nominal noise
  • Measure policy effectiveness empirically
  • Separate energy dependency from manufacturing performance
  • Communicate findings through rigorous visualisation

Question 1 of 7

Does Rupee depreciation narrow the trade deficit?

The Marshall-Lerner Condition

A foundational macroeconomic principle stating that a currency depreciation will improve the trade balance if and only if the sum of the price elasticities of exports and imports exceeds 1. A weaker currency makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports costlier domestically — theoretically reducing the trade deficit.

Our Hypothesis

India's persistent Rupee depreciation (from ₹20/USD in 1991 to ₹85+/USD today) should have progressively narrowed the trade deficit according to classical theory.

Context

Since liberalisation, the INR has depreciated by over 325% against the USD. Classical theory would predict this to be one of the most powerful forces correcting any trade imbalance.

Question 1 — Findings

Marshall-Lerner Condition: Empirically rejected for India

Finding 1 — Failure of Import Substitution

As the Rupee weakened over three decades, the physical volume of the trade deficit did not shrink — it expanded significantly. The two lines move in the same direction, not opposite directions as theory predicts.

Finding 2 — Inelastic Import Demand

India's core imports — predominantly crude oil, capital goods, and electronic components — cannot be substituted domestically. A weaker Rupee simply inflates the import bill without suppressing consumption.

⚠️

Conclusion: Relying on currency depreciation as a lever to achieve trade equilibrium is ineffective in the Indian context. A weaker Rupee actively harms the trade balance by inflating the cost of non-discretionary capital outflows.

Implication

Standard currency policy cannot substitute for structural reforms in domestic energy production, semiconductor manufacturing, and capital goods industries.

Question 2 of 7

Is India's deficit really an "energy deficit"?

The dominant narrative attributes India's persistent trade deficit to a broad manufacturing weakness and over-reliance on foreign consumer goods. But this ignores a critical structural reality.

India is the world's third-largest importer of crude oil — an inelastic energy commodity with virtually no domestic substitute at current production levels.

Our Hypothesis

When crude oil is stripped from the trade ledger, India's non-oil manufacturing sectors will demonstrate a trade balance significantly closer to equilibrium — suggesting the deficit is primarily an energy problem, not a manufacturing failure.

Why This Matters

If the deficit is energy-driven, policy prescriptions should focus on domestic energy transition (renewables, nuclear) rather than export incentives or currency interventions.

Question 2 — Findings

India's deficit is a structural energy problem, not a manufacturing failure

Finding 1 — Core Resilience of Non-Oil Trade

When crude oil is stripped from the ledger, the Non-Oil Trade Balance hovers remarkably close to zero. In several months, India's non-oil sectors actually ran a trade surplus.

Finding 2 — The Structural Energy Gap

The Overall Trade Balance remains deeply and persistently negative. The massive vertical gap between the two lines proves this is not a manufacturing shortfall — it is a structural, physical dependency on foreign energy.

💡

Conclusion: Characterising India's trade deficit as a broad failure of domestic manufacturing is empirically inaccurate. Stripped of petroleum imports, India's core merchandise trade demonstrates significant resilience and proximity to self-sustaining equilibrium.

Policy Implication

Accelerating India's renewable energy transition would have a far more dramatic impact on the trade balance than any manufacturing incentive scheme.

Question 3 of 7

How do global crises disrupt India's trade velocity?

Global supply chains are inherently vulnerable to macroeconomic crises. "Trade velocity" — the absolute aggregate volume of goods flowing in and out — is a powerful metric for measuring both the severity of disruption and the speed of recovery.

Three distinct historic events punctuated India's trade trajectory over our 35-year window.

Our Hypothesis

Each crisis will produce a distinct contraction signature. India's increasing global integration will make it more vulnerable to shocks over time, but also more elastic — recovering faster with each successive crisis.

Three Crisis Events Studied
  • 1991 — Balance of Payments Crisis & Economic Liberalisation
  • 2008 — Global Financial Crisis (Lehman collapse)
  • 2020 — COVID-19 Pandemic & energy demand crash

    Question 3 — Findings

    Three distinct shock signatures — each revealing deeper integration

    1991 — Structural Pivot

    Minimal absolute contraction but a permanent macroeconomic inflection point. Post-1992, both import and export trajectory slopes steepen definitively as India opens to global supply chains.

    2008 — V-Shaped Recovery

    Sharp synchronised contraction in both volumes. Despite the global liquidity freeze, India returned to its pre-crisis growth trajectory within 18–24 months — remarkable systemic elasticity.

    2020 — Asymmetric Collapse

    Most violent near-vertical collapse in 35 years. Imports fell harder than exports — driven by frozen domestic consumption and the historic crash in global energy demand.

    🔄

    Conclusion: India's modern trade matrix is deeply integrated and vulnerable to global contagion, but its physical manufacturing and consumption bases demonstrate high elasticity — consistently engineering rapid V-shaped recoveries following systemic global shocks.

    Question 4 of 7

    Do oil and non-oil trade exhibit distinct seasonal patterns?

    Macroeconomic trade data rarely distributes uniformly across a calendar year. Domestic fiscal policies, accounting deadlines, and cultural consumption patterns introduce distinct seasonal cycles.

    We hypothesised these fluctuations are driven entirely by discretionary non-oil merchandise (consumer electronics, gold, manufactured exports) — while inelastic energy imports should remain stable throughout the year.

    Our Hypothesis

    Oil trade volumes will be flat across all 12 months. Non-oil imports will spike before India's festive season (Q3), and non-oil exports will surge in March at India's fiscal year-end.

    Seasonal Events to Watch
    • March 31st — Indian fiscal year-end: export push to clear inventories
    • Oct–Nov — Dussehra & Diwali festive season: consumer import surge
    • Apr–Sep — Pre-festive inventory build by retailers
    Method

    Historical arithmetic mean calculated for each of 12 calendar months across 14 years (2011–2025), all in constant Dec 2025 USD.

    Question 4 — Findings

    India's trade seasonality is exclusively a non-oil phenomenon

    Finding 1 — Inelasticity of Energy Volumes

    Oil import and export trendlines remain largely flat across all 12 months. India's petroleum demand is structural and non-discretionary — unaffected by festivals or corporate calendars.

    Finding 2 — Pre-Festive Consumer Import Surge (Q3)

    Non-oil imports climb steadily from April, peaking in October — perfectly aligned with Dussehra and Diwali. Retailers aggressively front-load consumer durables, electronics, and gold imports.

    Finding 3 — Fiscal Year-End Export Spike (March)

    Non-oil exports spike significantly in March — manufacturers rush shipments to recognise revenue before the March 31st accounting closure.

    📅

    Conclusion: India inherently risks expanding its trade deficit in late summer and early autumn due to front-loaded festive inventory purchasing. This is predictably, albeit temporarily, offset by a surge in non-oil export velocity every March.

    Question 5 of 7

    Did "Make in India" accelerate export competitiveness?

    In September 2014, the Government of India launched "Make in India" — a flagship macroeconomic policy to transform the nation into a global design and manufacturing hub, attracting FDI and boosting exports.

    Objectively evaluating such policies requires careful methodological controls: excluding COVID-era volatility and deflating to constant USD to ensure physical manufacturing growth isn't confused with price inflation.

    Our Hypothesis

    The trajectory slope of inflation-adjusted non-oil exports will show a statistically significant upward acceleration in the post-2014 era compared to the pre-policy baseline.

    Two Eras Defined
  • Era 1 (Pre-Policy Baseline)
    Jan 2011 – Dec 2014
  • Era 2 (Make in India — Initial Phase)
    Jan 2015 – Dec 2019
    • Why Exclude 2020+?

    COVID-19 introduces massive exogenous volatility that would artificially skew trendline slopes, making it impossible to isolate the policy's genuine structural effect.

    Question 5 — Findings

    Make in India: A continuation, not a transformation

    Finding 1 — Stagnant Growth Velocity (CAGR)

    No meaningful acceleration in inflation-adjusted export CAGR. Era 2 (post-policy) failed to achieve a statistically higher breakout velocity — the policy did not significantly compound physical export volume.

    Finding 2 — Parallel Trajectory Slopes

    Visual inspection of the segmented regression reveals the Era 2 slope is remarkably flat and nearly parallel to the slope established during Era 1. No structural break is visible in the data.

    📉

    Conclusion: During its first five years, "Make in India" failed to radically steepen India's manufacturing competitiveness trajectory. Stripped of inflation and pandemic volatility, the policy's early footprint appears to be a mere linear continuation of pre-existing momentum rather than a paradigm-shifting industrial boom.

    Important Caveat

    Heavy manufacturing inherently requires long gestation periods. A 5-year post-launch window may capture implementation lags rather than the policy's ultimate structural efficacy.

    Question 6 of 7

    Is India's manufacturing becoming more self-reliant or more import-dependent?

    A common assumption in emerging market economics: growing export volumes signal a maturing, self-reliant manufacturing sector. But this is flawed if the sector exhibits high import elasticity.

    To produce one unit of export, if India must import a proportional amount of intermediate capital goods, semiconductors, or raw materials, then export growth simply amplifies import dependence — worsening the structural deficit.

    Our Hypothesis

    If manufacturing were becoming self-reliant, the physical CAGR of non-oil exports should substantially outpace non-oil imports. We expect to find the opposite — a widening structural gap.

    Import Elasticity Problem

    India's key non-oil imports include: semiconductor chips, active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), industrial machinery, and electronic components — all critical inputs for manufactured exports.

    India's non-oil sector is trapped in an import-dependency loop

    Non-oil Exports CAGR (2011–2024): 2.91%
    Non-oil Imports CAGR (2011–2024): 3.16%

    Because imports already had a larger absolute base, this small percentage gap translates into a massive absolute expansion of the structural deficit.

    Finding 2 — The Widening Structural Gap

    The absolute physical non-oil trade gap expanded by 62.49% between 2011 and 2024. The shaded ribbon in the chart grows wider and wider — unmistakably.

    🔗

    Conclusion: India's domestic merchandise manufacturing remains deeply and increasingly dependent on foreign capital goods and intermediate components. As India scales export volume, it structurally necessitates a disproportionate expansion of its import bill — trapping the non-oil trade balance in an import-dependency loop.

    Question 7 of 7

    How much of India's reported deficit "growth" is a currency illusion?

    Public policy and media narratives frequently cite the exponential expansion of India's trade deficit as evidence of severe macroeconomic deterioration.

    However, these reports overwhelmingly denominate the deficit in nominal Indian Rupees — a currency that has experienced persistent severe depreciation since 1991, while the global economy has simultaneously experienced decades of compounding price inflation.

    Our Hypothesis

    The apparent "explosion" of India's trade deficit is substantially a mathematical illusion driven by the denominator effect of currency devaluation and global inflation — not a pure physical expansion of the trade gap.

    Two Growth Vectors
  • Nominal INR Deficit — what is commonly reported in media and policy documents
  • Constant USD Deficit — adjusted for both exchange rate and global inflation (Dec 2025 USD base)
    • Method

    Base-100 index (1991 = 100) applied to both vectors, then plotted together to reveal the divergence in perceived vs. real deficit growth.

    Question 7 — Findings

    The deficit grew ~4,100% in real terms — but 46,100% in nominal Rupees

    Finding 1 — True Physical Growth (Constant USD)

    Measured in inflation-adjusted US Dollars, the physical magnitude of the trade deficit grew by roughly ~4,100% from 1991 to 2025. Notable, but relatively linear in trajectory.

    Finding 2 — The Nominal INR Illusion

    The exact same physical trade gap, when measured in unadjusted nominal Indian Rupees, appears to have grown by an astronomical ~46,100%. The parabolic curve in the chart is currency depreciation and inflation, not physical deterioration.

    Limitations

    Constraints on the analysis — what we cannot claim

    📦 Approximation of Physical Volume

    Nominal values were deflated by US CPI, but the dataset lacks pure physical volume indices (tonnage, unit counts). CPI is a robust proxy but cannot perfectly isolate price effects from quantity effects across heterogeneous import sectors.

    🏭 Merchandise Bias

    Only physical merchandise trade is tracked. India runs a massive services export surplus (IT, BPO, consulting) — analysing only merchandise significantly overstates the nation's true current account deficit.

    📊 Aggregation of Non-Oil Commodities

    The "Non-Oil" category remains heavily aggregated. Surges cannot be attributed to specific sectors (e.g., electronics vs. gold), limiting the granularity of domestic manufacturing assessments.

    🌐 Bilateral Trade Obscurity

    Data is aggregated at the global macro level — no partner-country breakdowns. Geopolitical dependencies and bilateral asymmetries (e.g., India's merchandise deficit with China) cannot be assessed.

    ⛽ Price vs. Quantity in Energy

    Even with Oil/Non-Oil disaggregation, only aggregate monetary values are available. It is impossible to definitively separate crude oil demand surges (volume) from global supply shocks (price).

    ⏳ Policy Implementation Lags

    The Make in India evaluation is bounded by the pre-2020 window. Heavy manufacturing infrastructure requires substantial gestation. A 5-year window may capture implementation lags rather than ultimate structural efficacy.
